# The Unhurried Traveller's Guide to the Sovereign Republic of Brindlemark

Welcome, weary wanderer, to Brindlemark — a nation that exists nowhere on any
map you have ever consulted, which is precisely why your luggage always arrives
on time. Brindlemark is a small federation of misty highlands, lavender lowlands,
and at least four lakes that refuse to stay the same shape from one census to the
next. Here you will find no traffic, no queues longer than three citizens, and a
national obsession with the correct way to fold a napkin. This guide will prepare
you for everything Brindlemark may throw at you, including the weather, the
pastries, and the famously argumentative geese.

## A Word Before You Pack

Brindlemarkers (never "Brindlemarkians" — that is a grievous insult dating back
to the Treaty of Soggy Meadows) believe that a traveller arrives twice: first in
body, and second in spirit, roughly two days later, once the body has finished
complaining about the journey. They therefore recommend you plan to do nothing
of consequence for your first forty-eight hours. Sit. Drink the tea. Watch the
clouds, which here are considered a legitimate form of regional entertainment and
are reviewed weekly in the national newspaper under the heading "Sky of the Week."

The official languages are Brindish, Old Brindish, and Emphatic Gesturing. Most
citizens speak passable Traveller's Common, especially if you compliment the
weather first. The currency is the brindle, subdivided into one hundred marks,
though in rural districts you may still be asked to pay in pleasant conversation,
useful gossip, or a song nobody has heard before.

## The Capital City of Velmoria

Every journey through Brindlemark begins, sensibly, in the capital city of
Velmoria, a settlement so old that its founding date is recorded as "a Tuesday,
probably." Velmoria sprawls across seven gentle hills, each named after a
different breakfast food, and the locals navigate not by street signs — of which
there are famously none — but by reference to which hill smells most strongly of
which pastry at any given hour.

Velmoria is the seat of government, the heart of commerce, and the only place in
the nation where you can reliably buy a left shoe without committing to the right
one. It is a city of crooked towers, copper rooftops gone green with age, and an
unshakeable conviction that everything was better in a decade nobody can quite
specify. The Velmorians are proud, talkative, and constitutionally incapable of
giving directions in fewer than four landmarks, two opinions, and one story about
their aunt.

The undisputed jewel of the capital is the river that runs through it: the
Quillsong. The Quillsong winds through the centre of Velmoria like a ribbon
dropped by a careless giant, and the entire civic life of the city is organised
around its banks. The river takes its name, locals insist, from the sound it
makes as it passes beneath the Old Stone Bridge — a soft scratching murmur, as if
a thousand tiny pens were writing letters no one will ever read. Whether the
Quillsong sounds like quills or whether the citizens have simply decided that it
does is a question best not raised in polite company, as it has ended more than
one friendship and at least one marriage.

Along the Quillsong you will find the floating markets, the singing laundresses,
the Guild of Reluctant Ferrymen, and the famous Lantern Walk, where every
evening at dusk the riverbank glows with ten thousand paper lanterns, each one
inscribed with a wish, a complaint, or a recipe. It is considered extremely bad
luck to read someone else's lantern aloud, and extremely good luck to leave one
of your own.

## Getting Around Velmoria

There are precisely three ways to travel within Velmoria: on foot, by boat along
the Quillsong, or via the Municipal Goose Carriage Service, which is exactly as
chaotic and as delightful as it sounds. The geese are not, technically, employed
by the city — they have simply decided, over several centuries, that pulling
small carriages is an acceptable use of their morning. They demand payment in
breadcrumbs and respect, and they keep impeccable schedules, which is more than
can be said for the ferrymen.

A gentle warning about the geese: they are intelligent, opinionated, and they
remember faces. The traveller who shortchanges a goose on Monday will find that
same goose waiting, with a slow and judgmental waddle, outside their lodging on
Tuesday. Pay your geese. Tip your geese. Compliment the plumage of your geese.
This is not superstition; it is sound personal-safety advice.

If you prefer the water, hire a flat-bottomed riverboat and drift the Quillsong
from the Hill of Warm Buttered Toast down to the Hill of Suspiciously Good Jam.
The current is lazy, the ferrymen are reluctant (it is in their guild charter),
and the journey takes precisely as long as it takes. Bring a hat. Bring snacks.
Bring a willingness to be told, repeatedly, that the river was wider in the old
days.

## The Seven Hills of Velmoria

**Toast Hill** is the highest and the proudest, crowned by the Parliament of
Polite Disagreement, where the nation's laws are debated, amended, and quite
often simply withdrawn out of embarrassment. Visitors may observe sessions from
the public gallery, provided they promise not to clap, boo, or audibly sigh.

**Jam Hill** is the artistic quarter, home to painters, poets, and people who
describe themselves as "between projects." Its winding lanes are lined with
galleries selling art so abstract that several pieces have been hung upside down
for decades without anyone noticing or, frankly, minding.

**Porridge Hill** is the scholarly district, dominated by the Great Library of
Velmoria, whose collection is so vast that an entire profession — the Pathfinders
— exists solely to help readers find a book and then find their way back out
again. The library has no catalogue; it has a tradition, which is considered far
more reliable.

**Honey Hill** is the merchants' quarter, all bustle and bargaining, where you
can purchase anything from a wheel of weeping cheese to a map of a country that
may or may not exist. Haggling is not merely permitted here; it is mandatory, and
to accept the first price offered is considered deeply rude to the seller, who
was looking forward to the negotiation.

**Pastry Hill** is, predictably, where the bakers live, and the smell alone has
been known to reduce hardened travellers to tears. We shall return to the matter
of pastries shortly, because it deserves its own solemn section.

**Egg Hill** is the residential district, a tangle of narrow houses leaning
companionably against one another like old friends after a long dinner. It is
here you will find the true Velmoria: washing strung between windows, children
chasing the carriage-geese, and grandmothers conducting feuds across the alley
that have outlasted three separate governments.

**Kipper Hill**, the seventh and smallest, is where nobody quite admits to
living, and which appears on no two maps in the same location. The Velmorians
speak of it fondly but vaguely. Do not go looking for Kipper Hill. Kipper Hill,
they say, finds you.

## On the Sacred Subject of Pastries

It would be a dereliction of duty to send any traveller into Brindlemark without
adequate pastry preparation. The national pastry is the *flummel*, a spiralled,
many-layered confection that takes a master baker eleven years to learn and the
average eater eleven seconds to finish. Flummels come in two varieties: sweet and
"complicated," and the difference between them is a closely guarded secret that
each baker takes to the grave, often dramatically and with witnesses.

Etiquette demands that a flummel be eaten from the outside in, unspooling the
spiral as you go, while maintaining eye contact with whoever gave it to you. To
eat a flummel from the centre outward marks you instantly as a foreigner, a
philistine, or a spy. To refuse a flummel altogether is grounds for the host to
assume you have died and to begin planning the funeral.

Beyond the flummel lies a glittering universe of regional baked goods: the
honeyed *grumblecake* of the southern marshes, the alarmingly dense *anvil bun*
of the mining towns, and the legendary *whisper tart* of Velmoria itself, so
delicate that it must be eaten in a soundproofed room lest a loud noise cause it
to collapse into sugar dust. Yes, this is real. No, we cannot explain it. Eat the
tart in the silent room and do not ask questions.

## Festivals and Reasons to Avoid Bureaucracy

Brindlemark observes more festivals than there are days in the year, which the
government has solved by holding several at once and trusting the citizens to
sort it out. The greatest of these is the Festival of the Turning Leaf, held each
autumn along the banks of the Quillsong in Velmoria, when the entire city floats
candlelit boats downstream and pretends, for one night, that summer is not over.
It is unbearably beautiful, and every Brindlemarker over the age of forty will
tell you it was more beautiful when they were young.

Spring brings the Festival of Loud Apologies, during which citizens publicly
forgive one another for the slights of the previous year, a tradition that has
single-handedly prevented at least four civil wars and caused approximately two
hundred awkward dinner parties. Summer brings the Regatta of Reluctant Ferrymen,
in which the ferrymen of the Quillsong race one another with visible reluctance,
the prize being the right to be left alone for the rest of the season.

Winter brings the Long Quiet, a fortnight during which all of Brindlemark agrees,
collectively and without coordination, to simply stop. Shops close. Carriages
rest. Even the argumentative geese fall silent. Travellers caught in the Long
Quiet are advised to find a warm room, a stack of flummels, and a good book from
the Great Library, and to make peace with doing absolutely nothing at all. Many
visitors report it as the finest fortnight of their lives.

## The Lavender Lowlands

South of Velmoria, beyond the last of the seven hills, the land softens into the
Lavender Lowlands, an endless rolling sea of purple that perfumes the air for
miles. The villages here are small, sleepy, and almost suspiciously content. The
lowlanders keep bees the size of your thumb, produce honey that has been known to
make grown diplomats weep, and measure wealth not in brindles but in the number
of jars stacked in one's cellar.

The lowland capital — if a village of two hundred souls and four hundred geese
can be called a capital — is Mossgrove, reachable from Velmoria by a half-day
drift down the Quillsong followed by a short and heavily negotiated cart ride.
Mossgrove is famous for exactly one thing: the Whispering Hedge, a maze of ancient
lavender so tall and so old that travellers who enter it emerge, hours later,
having apparently resolved every problem in their lives. Nobody knows how. The
lowlanders refuse to discuss it and simply hand you another jar of honey.

## The Misty Highlands

To the north, the land rears up into the Misty Highlands, where the air is thin,
the sheep are philosophical, and the weather changes its mind roughly every nine
minutes. This is the wild heart of Brindlemark, a place of stone villages,
windswept passes, and inns where the fire never goes out and the soup never runs
dry. The highlanders are a hardy, laconic people who consider a sentence of more
than six words to be showing off.

The highlands are home to the Singing Stones of Cair Dolloch, a circle of
standing stones that hum faintly when the wind blows from the west, and not at all
when anyone is paying attention. Scholars from the Great Library have studied the
stones for centuries and produced exactly one agreed-upon conclusion: that they
are, in fact, stones. The highlanders find this research hilarious.

Here too you will find the source of the great river itself. High in the pass of
Glen Mirrow, a spring bubbles up from beneath a flat grey rock, and from this
modest beginning the Quillsong sets out on its long journey south, gathering
streams and stories until it reaches Velmoria and the sea beyond. Pilgrims climb
to the source each year to whisper a single word into the water and watch it
carried away. The highlanders watch the pilgrims, shake their heads, and charge
them a modest fee for parking.

## Lodging, and the Art of the Brindish Inn

The Brindish inn is an institution unto itself. You do not book a room; you are
assigned one, by an innkeeper who has assessed your character in the first three
seconds and matched you to the chamber they feel you deserve. Travellers of good
humour are given the rooms with the view of the Quillsong. Travellers who
complain about the journey are given the room above the kitchen, next to the
goose pen.

Breakfast is included, enormous, and non-negotiable. You will eat the eggs. You
will eat the grumblecake. You will accept a second helping, and a third, and you
will be sent off into the day so thoroughly fed that lunch becomes a distant and
theoretical concern. To leave a Brindish breakfast hungry is not possible; the
innkeepers regard it as a personal failure and have been known to follow guests
into the street with additional toast.

## Practical Matters and Gentle Warnings

A few final notes for the prudent traveller. First: tipping. In Brindlemark one
does not tip with money, which is considered crass, but with a small, true
compliment, delivered sincerely. The boatman who ferries you down the Quillsong
will treasure "your steering was as smooth as warm honey" far more than any coin.

Second: photography. The Velmorians are happy to be photographed but believe,
sincerely, that a photograph captures a small portion of one's luck, and so they
will insist on photographing you back, to make it even. Carry a means of being
photographed. It is only polite.

Third, and most importantly: the geese. We have mentioned the geese. We will
mention them again. Respect the geese. The geese run the carriages, guard the
bridges, and — many suspect — quietly run the country. The Parliament of Polite
Disagreement has, on three separate occasions, attempted to pass legislation
regulating the geese, and on all three occasions the relevant documents were
found, the following morning, shredded and arranged into a rude shape on the
steps of Toast Hill. Draw your own conclusions. Pay your geese.

## A Brief and Disputed History

No guide to Brindlemark would be complete without a history, though every
Brindlemarker will tell you a different one, usually louder than the last. What
follows is the version printed in the official school textbooks, which means it
is wrong in at least four interesting ways that locals will be delighted to
correct you on over dinner.

In the beginning, the textbooks say, there was the Quillsong, and along its banks
came the First Settlers, a wandering people who had grown tired of being chased
out of grander, more ambitious nations. They liked the river. They liked the
hills. They liked, above all, that nobody else seemed to want the place. So they
stayed, planted lavender, befriended the geese — or, more accurately, surrendered
to them — and got on with the serious business of perfecting the flummel.

For several centuries Brindlemark had no government at all, an arrangement the
citizens still speak of wistfully. Disputes were settled by the oldest person in
the room, and when that failed, by the loudest. Eventually the population grew
large enough that a Parliament became necessary, chiefly so that there would be
somewhere official to disagree, rather than disagreeing everywhere at once, which
had become exhausting.

The defining moment of the national story is the aforementioned Treaty of Soggy
Meadows, signed after the only war Brindlemark has ever fought, which lasted a
single afternoon and was caused entirely by a misunderstanding over the correct
recipe for grumblecake. No one was harmed. Several feelings were. The treaty
established three eternal principles: that the geese are not to be regulated, that
the flummel is to be eaten outside-in, and that nobody is ever, under any
circumstances, to be called a Brindlemarkian. These principles remain the
bedrock of the constitution to this day.

## The Calendar, Roughly Speaking

Brindlemark keeps a calendar of thirteen months, the thirteenth of which —
Smudgewill — exists only in years when the citizens collectively agree that the
year has been long enough already and could do with another month to recover.
This is decided by a show of hands in the Parliament of Polite Disagreement, and
in practice Smudgewill arrives roughly two years in three.

The week has six days: Toilsday, Restday, Anotherday, Marketday, Geeseday, and
Slowday. Geeseday is a public holiday in honour of the carriage-geese, who take
the day off whether it is granted to them or not. Slowday is the day on which
nothing may be hurried, contracts may not be signed, and any decision made is
considered legally non-binding and probably ill-advised. Visitors are strongly
encouraged to do their sightseeing on Slowday, when the whole nation moves at the
pace of the drifting Quillsong.

## Words You Will Need

A small glossary, offered in the spirit of survival:

- *Flummel*: the national pastry; see above; eat it correctly or leave.
- *Brindle*: the currency, and also a colour, and also a mood, depending on tone.
- *Quillsong*: the great river running through the capital city of Velmoria; the
  beating heart of the nation; do not fall in, the ferrymen will be reluctant to
  fish you out.
- *Grumblecake*: a honeyed cake of the southern marshes; the cause of the only
  war in national history.
- *Goose*: deceptively simple word for a creature of terrifying competence.
- *Smudgeworthy*: an adjective of the highest praise, meaning a thing so good it
  justifies adding an extra month to the year.
- *Reluctant*: the default emotional state of any ferryman on the Quillsong.

When in doubt, smile, gesture broadly, and compliment the weather. It has yet to
fail anyone.

## The Cuisine Beyond Pastry

Lest you imagine Brindlemarkers live on flummels alone — though several have
tried — the national cuisine extends well beyond the bakery. The signature dish
is *riverpot*, a slow-simmered stew of whatever the Quillsong has provided that
week, thickened with barley and seasoned with a herb the highlanders refuse to
name. It is served in a communal pot at the centre of the table, and the unspoken
rule is that you must tell a story before you take a second ladle. The longer the
story, the larger the portion you are permitted. Brindlemark thus has both the
best-fed and the most talkative dinner guests in the known world.

Cheese is taken seriously to the point of solemnity. The famous weeping cheese of
Honey Hill is aged in cellars along the Quillsong and is said to absorb the murmur
of the river as it matures, giving it a flavour the locals describe, unhelpfully,
as "like remembering something nice." There is also a blue-veined variety so
pungent that it is traditionally stored in a separate building and approached only
on special occasions and from upwind.

To drink, there is river tea — leaves steeped in water drawn from the Quillsong at
dawn — and, for the bold, a fermented honey beverage called *sting* that the
lowlanders produce and the highlanders pretend not to enjoy. Sting is served in
thimble-sized cups, for reasons that become abundantly clear after the second
one.

## A Fond Farewell

And so we leave you on the banks of the Quillsong, in the heart of Velmoria, with
a flummel in one hand and a paper lantern in the other, watching the clouds drift
over the seven hills as the carriage-geese settle in for the night. Brindlemark
asks little of its visitors: only that you slow down, eat well, speak kindly, and
never, ever, eat your flummel from the centre out.

Travel gently. The country, like the river, is in no hurry whatsoever — and
neither, after a few days here, will you be.
